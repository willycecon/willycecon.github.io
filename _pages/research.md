## Job Market Paper
<div class="paper-entry">
  <a href="https://willycecon.com/JMP.pdf"><strong>Discounted Degrees: Employer Frictions and the Misallocation of High-Skilled Immigrants</strong></a>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Abstract</summary> 
Immigrants in the US are more often over-educated for their jobs than natives, but the reason is not well understood. I study one explanation: a cost that employers bear when they hire an
immigrant. When firms cannot pay an immigrant less than a native in the same job, the cost falls on the job instead of the wage. In a signaling model with this constraint, immigrants keep
their degrees and take jobs that require less education, and the gap shrinks with the degree. I test these predictions with a LinkedIn panel that follows college graduates across countries. I find that in their first US job, immigrant master’s holders are 3.1 percentage points more likely than comparable US-educated workers to be over-educated if they hold a US degree and 9.1 points more likely if they hold only foreign degrees. The gap is smaller for doctorate holders and largest, 20.9 points, for foreign-trained physicians, lawyers, and other professionals who must relicense. Among workers who stay, foreign-trained doctorate and professional-degree holders start in occupations that pay less than those of comparable US-educated workers and close that deficit within ten years, whereas master’s holders keep a small premium. The 2008 extension of STEM Optional Practical Training lengthened work authorization without changing training. It raised the share of immigrant STEM master’s graduates who started their careers in the US by 3.7 percentage points, and those who stayed more often worked in their field and held occupations paying about 8 log points more, although over-education did not fall. The results suggest that at least part of the hiring cost is created by visa policy and can be removed by it.
