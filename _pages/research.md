## Job Market Paper
<div class="paper-entry">
  <a href="https://willycecon.com/JMP.pdf"><strong>Discounted Degrees: Employer Frictions and the Misallocation of High-Skilled Immigrants</strong></a>

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Abstract</summary> 
Immigrant over-education in the United States is well documented, but its mechanism is not. I develop a signaling model in which an employer friction, together with rules that bar paying an immigrant less than a native in the same job, moves the adjustment from the wage to the job: immigrants keep their degrees and take jobs that require less education, by less at higher degrees. Using a new LinkedIn panel that follows college graduates across borders, I find that at their first US job, immigrant master's holders with a US degree and with only foreign degrees are $4.1$ and $8.5$ percentage points more likely to be over-educated than comparable US-educated workers. The gap is smaller for doctorate holders, and positive for those with a US degree at employers subject to the H-1B cap; it is largest, $16.0$ points, for foreign-trained professionals such as physicians and lawyers, who must relicense. Over a decade, the occupational wage penalty specific to foreign degrees halves for doctorate and professional-degree holders but narrows by only a fifth for master's holders. The 2008 STEM Optional Practical Training extension, which lengthened work authorization without changing training, raised US career starts among 2008--2011 STEM master's graduates by $3.7$ percentage points, and stayers moved into their field and into higher-paying occupations, mostly at employers subject to the H-1B lottery. The friction has more than one source, and part of it is a cost that policy can remove, not a productivity gap.
</details>
